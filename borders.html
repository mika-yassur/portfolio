(Un)Envisioning Future Borders: Environmental Divisions in Gaza

Conducting a Normalized Difference Enhanced Sand Index on the area that constitutes Gaza in 2020 reveals a thin blue border outlining the strip. Through this spectral analysis and false color composite, pixels rendered in blue and green indicate a spectral signature of sand and arid land. This borderland, which has dictated the supply of essential goods into Gaza along with the restriction of all persons moving in and out of the region, is also an ecological and environmental phenomenon, physically inscribed into the earth.

In attempting to explain the historical evolution and significance of this border I turn to scholars Andrew Herscher and Ana María León, who emphasize the colonial origins of borders in their text “At the Border of Decolonization.” Under colonialism, they write, “two functions of the border are paramount: borders not only inscribe colonialism into the Earth, parceling land into property, but they also legitimize this inscription, as colonists pose Indigenous land without the borders they recognized as ‘empty.’” These processes of bordering and enclosure are facilitated, the authors note, through the grid of urban planning and the map. Through these lenses, we can view the border as a “colonial geo-epistemology that encompasses gridding, separating land and water, and the delineation of property lines.” In other words, colonial ways of knowing are representationally and materially inscribed through processes of bordering and circumscription. These inscriptions can thus be read retroactively as “historical artifacts of a colonial episteme.” This understanding prompts a genealogical examination of the production of these ways of seeing—as Herscher and León assert, “neglecting the colonial genealogy of the border renders border work of any and all sort as a deferral of the work of decolonization.” Following this, this essay attempts to construct a partial genealogy of Gaza’s borderlands, of which there have been and continue to be many.

Drawing on Herscher and León, I focus on the construction of the border not only as the formation of property regimes and colonial land expropriation but primarily as the cartographical and epistemological designation of environmental zones consisting of sand or soil. By constructing borders along and with environmental divisions, I argue, colonial states seek to further naturalize their formation. In the case of the British Mandate and later the Israeli state, this naturalization is deeply entangled with colonial temporal discourses that project these borders, and colonial ontologies of land more broadly, into the past and future. By tracing the history of these borders across colonial projects, I seek precisely to unsettle this enduring temporal premise, asking how environmental divisions come to constitute borders in settler colonial contexts, and how settler colonial borders become environmentally defined.
